# Corpus sweep: Tolstoy and the Swedes, 1895–1910 (for the Betänken Eder! dive)

Source: `primary-sources/tolstoydigital-TEI/texts/` (PSS ed., Moscow 1928–58). Extracts were made with `extract_tei.py --choice=reg --notes=auto`, saved as `<tei-id>.txt`. Where the editorial commentary under a letter matters, a second copy made with `--notes=force` is saved as `<tei-id>.notes-force.txt` (the `auto` mode drops letter footnotes). Every Russian snippet below is copied from the named file; each is marked `[v70_175_RedaktorugazetyStokholmTagblatt.txt]`.

## Headline result

- The corpus contains **nothing** that connects *Одумайтесь!* (1904) to Sweden: not in the Tom 36 commentary, not in the 1904 letters (Tom 75, Tom 88), not in the 1904 diaries (Tom 55). No Swedish translator, no Björck & Börjesson, no peace society is named for 1904.
- The corpus also contains **no hit at all** for Arnoldson, Wavrinsky, Svenska freds- och skiljedomsföreningen, Björck, Ellen Key or Langlet-as-peace-man. (The only Arnoldson hit is a 1891 note about a Bjørnson preface, rejected below.)
- What the corpus does document is the earlier Swedish thread: the 1899 collective letter from Stockholm (Riksdag members, professors, pastors) and Tolstoy's reply, translated and printed by Jonas Stadling; the 1897 Nobel/Dukhobor letter to Swedish papers, translated with Voldemar Langlet; the 1902 greeting from Swedish writers; the 1909 and 1910 invitations to the Stockholm peace congress; and a family tie (son Lev Lvovich's Swedish wife, from 1896).
- Pattern for 1904 foreign editions: Chertkov (Free Word Press, England) controlled them. His 1904 letter says the article is translated into English "and will appear simultaneously in different countries". A Swedish edition most likely came through that channel, but the corpus does not say so.

### 3. v90_060_066: "По поводу конгресса о мире. Письмо к шведам" (Tom 90, pp. 60–66), final text, January 1899
- File: `v90_060_066_Po_povodu_kongressa_o_mire_Pismo_k_shvedam.txt`.
- RU: `Мысль, высказанная в прекрасном письме вашем о том, что всеобщее разоружение может быть достигнуто самым легким и верным путем посредством отказа отдельных лиц от участия в военной службе, — совершенно справедлива.` [v90_060_066_Po_povodu_kongressa_o_mire_Pismo_k_shvedam.txt]
- (working English) The idea expressed in your fine letter, that general disarmament can be reached most easily and surely through individuals refusing military service, is entirely correct.
- RU: `Конференция может только отвести глаза народа от единственного средства спасения и освобождения.` [same file]

He re-read this text in 1909 as his preparation for Stockholm (item 9). The same refusal-of-service argument is the core of *Одумайтесь!*.

This explains why a peace society might still want to publish him (his harshest anti-war text) even though he dismisses their method.

## Open questions
1. Who translated *Одумайтесь!* into Swedish and who proposed it? Nothing in Tom 36, Tom 55, Tom 75 or Tom 88 names a Swedish translator, publisher (Björck & Börjesson) or peace society. Check Chertkov's Free Word Press records (Tolstoy–Chertkov correspondence, not fully in this corpus), Swedish archives (SFSF minutes, Björck & Börjesson list) and Birukoff's *Biography* vol. IV.
2. Did SFSF people (Arnoldson, Wavrinsky, Bonde, Bergman) belong to the 1899 signatory group? The commentary lists only occupations (four Riksdag members, pastors, professors...), not names. The Russian text from *Svobodnaya Mysl* 1899, 1 and Stadling's Swedish print in *Aftonbladet* would show names.
3. Who asked Tolstoy for the 1899 letter? The corpus says a "collective letter from Stockholm" signed by the occupations above; Stadling is the contact who received Tolstoy's reply and forwarded/translated it. The original Swedish letter was not found in the archive ("Подлинник письма шведов в архиве не обнаружен"). Whether Stadling himself organised it is not stated.
4. How did the Swedish Peace and Arbitration Society relate to Stadling, Langlet and the 1899 group? Not in the corpus; needs Swedish sources.
5. What does the Swedish edition's cover phrase "Till krig emot kriget!" correspond to in the Russian? Not found in the Russian text; likely the Swedish publisher's or society's slogan. Check against the Swedish edition itself.
6. Was the Swedish edition made from the first Free Word edition (with epigraphs) or from the popular edition for soldiers (without)? The Tom 36 commentary records both and a third; the Swedish edition's front matter should say.
7. Voldemar Langlet's later role (translator 1897; Swedish translator of Tolstoy in later years?) is not followed up in the corpus; worth a check against Swedish sources.
