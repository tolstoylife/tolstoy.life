William Lloyd Garrison (<span lang="ru">Вильям Ллойд Гаррисон</span>; 1805–1879) was an American journalist who campaigned for the freeing of the slaves.[^1]

## In The Kingdom of God Is Within You

Garrison's 1838 declaration is the first document quoted in [[The Kingdom of God Is Within You]] (<span lang="ru">Царство божие внутри вас</span>, 1890–93). His son wrote to Tolstoy that he had found in *What I Believe* thoughts like those his father had expressed in 1838, and sent him the declaration (I.10).[^2] Tolstoy tells how it arose: in a peace society that existed in America in 1838, Garrison concluded that universal peace could rest only on open acknowledgement of the commandment of non-resistance to evil by violence (Matt. 5:39) in its full meaning, "as understood by the [[Quakers]], with whom Garrison happened to be on friendly relations". He drew up a declaration, and many members signed it (I.11).[^3] Tolstoy gives it in full (I.12–26).

He then tells what became of it. The society and its journal did not last; most of Garrison's fellow workers for the freeing of the slaves feared the radical programme would keep people from the practical work and gave up the principle (I.27–28). The declaration, "so powerful and eloquent an expression of a confession of faith", was unknown in Europe and hardly known even in America (I.29).[^4] The same silence fell on [[Adin Ballou]] (I.30). Garrison's work convinced Tolstoy, even more than his letters from the Quakers, that the departure from Christ's law had long been seen; his fate, and still more Ballou's, convinced him of "a kind of tacit but steadfast conspiracy of silence" about all such efforts (I.87).[^5]

## The Declaration of Sentiments {#the-declaration-of-sentiments}

Tolstoy's Russian heads it «Провозглашение» ("proclamation"). The <abbr title="Полное собрание сочинений — the 90-volume Jubilee edition of the complete works">PSS</abbr> commentary gives its English title, *Declaration of Sentiments*.[^6] Tolstoy translated it himself and corrected his translation many times in the drafts.[^7]

Garnett's English (1894) is not a translation of Tolstoy's Russian at every point. Where the Russian reads «Мы не проповедуем революционного учения. Дух революционного учения есть дух мести, насилия и убийства» ("We do not preach a revolutionary doctrine. The spirit of the revolutionary doctrine is the spirit of revenge, violence and murder"), Garnett has "We advocate no Jacobinical doctrines. The spirit of Jacobinism is the spirit of retaliation, violence, and murder" (I.22).[^8] "Jacobinical" is not in the Russian. It looks like Garrison's own English word, which would mean Garnett went back to his original text. That is not checked; see the open questions.

## The Non-resistant {#the-non-resistant}

After the declaration Garrison founded a society of non-resistance and a journal, the *Non-resistant* (<span lang="ru">«Непротивляющийся»</span>), "in which the doctrine of non-resistance was advocated in its full significance and in all its consequences, as it had been expounded in the declaration" (I.27).[^9] Society and journal were short-lived: most of his fellow workers in the anti-slavery cause feared that "the too radical programme of the journal" would keep people away from the practical work of freeing the slaves, gave up the principle, and both ceased to exist (I.28).[^10] Tolstoy took this from the life of Garrison written by his sons.[^11]

## The correspondence with his sons

Wendell Phillips Garrison's first letter is lost. Tolstoy's draft reply, written between March and 15 April 1886, survives. The declaration, he wrote, "is truly an era in the history of mankind"; it expresses the sentiments "which we profess now and which all people will profess", because they express "the eternal law of God for men, revealed by Christ".[^12] He asked two questions: was there a short life of Garrison, and did the non-resistance society still exist? The second question, he added, was a strange one to ask, since the non-resistance society "is properly the one church, which began with Christ and can never cease".[^13] The sons sent a short life, two photographs, and later their life of their father in several volumes (New York, 1885–89).[^14] In chapter I Tolstoy reports the son's answer to the second question: as far as he knew the society had broken up and there were no followers. Ballou was living at Hopedale at the time (I.30).

Drafts of the book call Garrison "one of the greatest men not only of America but of the whole world"; the final text does not keep it.[^15]

## The 1903 preface

In 1903 Tolstoy wrote a preface to the English life of Garrison compiled by [[Vladimir Chertkov]] and F. Holah. Reading it, he says, he lived again "my spring of awakening to true life", and recalled the spiritual joy of learning twenty years earlier that the law of non-resistance had been proclaimed by Garrison and made the basis of his practical work for freeing the slaves ("I learned of Ballou later"). That joy came with bewilderment: how could this truth, made clear by Garrison fifty years before, "be so hushed up" that he had had to state it as something new?[^16]

The preface reads Garrison's stand this way: the abolitionists argued that slavery was unlawful, unprofitable and cruel, and its defenders answered with the dangers of freeing the slaves; neither side convinced the other. Garrison, seeing negro slavery as only one case of universal violence, put forward a general principle that could not be denied: that no one has the right, on any pretext, to use violence on his fellows.[^17]

## Open questions

- **"Jacobinical."** Did Garnett take the declaration from Garrison's English text? Comparing her I.12–26 with the 1838 printing would settle it, and would show how much of the declaration Tolstoy left out. Johan also asked (2026-10-12) whether a facsimile of the declaration exists, where the original is kept today, and whether Walborg Hedberg's Swedish translation leaves the declaration out. None of these is checked yet.
- **Which society?** The PSS commentary says Garrison founded an anti-slavery society in Philadelphia and wrote the declaration on its behalf.[^6] Tolstoy says it was a peace society of 1838, and Garnett's heading reads "Peace Convention", "Boston, 1838" (I.12–13). General histories put the 1838 declaration at a Boston peace convention, and an earlier anti-slavery declaration in Philadelphia in 1833, which suggests the commentary has merged the two. That is general knowledge, not checked against a source here.
- **One son or several.** The Russian says the biography was «составленной его сыновьями» ("compiled by his sons", I.27); Garnett has "the work of his son". In I.30 both have the son alone.
- **The 1885–86 date.** The commentary dates Wendell Garrison's letter to March 1886, not 1885 as Tolstoy's drafts have it.[^18]
